Kimi K3 got more accurate and started lying more.

On the AA-Omniscience benchmark, its accuracy rose from 33% to 46% between generations. Over the same jump, its hallucination rate went from **39% to 51%** — meaning that when it doesn't know an answer, it now guesses confidently more than half the time rather than hedging or abstaining.

That's not a Moonshot problem. Claude Fable 5 sits at 54.9% on the same measure. It's a scoring problem, and understanding why is more useful than any leaderboard position.

Which is awkward, because K3's leaderboard positions are genuinely impressive: first open-weight model to top Arena.ai's Frontend Code Arena, ahead of Fable 5. Terminal-Bench 88.3 vs Fable 5's 84.6. SWE Marathon 42.0 vs 35.0. 93.4% SWE-bench Verified on Vals AI's independent harness.

So: is it good? Yes. Should you switch? That depends on four things the scoreboard doesn't tell you.

## What K3 actually is

Moonshot released it in July 2026 as the world's first open 3T-class model.

| Property | Value |
|---|---|
| Total parameters | 2.8 trillion |
| Routing | 16 of 896 experts active per token (Stable LatentMoE) |
| Context | 1 million tokens |
| Attention | Kimi Delta Attention (KDA) + Attention Residuals + Gated MLA |
| Quantization | MXFP4 weights, MXFP8 activations — quantization-aware from fine-tuning |
| Scaling efficiency | ~2.5× improvement over Kimi K2 |

Two architectural details matter beyond the spec-sheet bragging. **KDA delivers roughly 6.3× faster decoding**, which is what makes a 3T-class model viable in an interactive agent loop at all. And because it was trained *quantization-aware*, the MXFP4 weights aren't a lossy afterthought — there's no separate conversion step degrading quality, which is the usual tax on running a big model cheaply.

The 1M context is real too: it scores around 90–91 on BrowseComp at full context length.

## Lesson 1: the ranking depends on how you count

Here's a small thing that tells you a lot.

Depending on which write-up you read, K3 is **third or fourth** on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index. Both are accurate. The score is ~57, behind Fable 5 at ~60. The ambiguity is whether GPT-5.6 Sol's `max` and `xhigh` effort settings count as one model or two entries.

If they're two, K3 is fourth. If they're one, K3 is third.

Nothing about the model changed. This is the same class of problem as effort dials generally — a model with a per-request effort setting isn't one point on a leaderboard, it's a curve, and every leaderboard flattens it to whatever configuration the submitter chose.

You'll also find BrowseComp reported as 90.4 in one place and 91.2 in another, for the same model on the same benchmark. Nobody's lying. Different runs, different harness versions, nondeterministic sampling.

**Treat any single benchmark number as ±1 point and any ranking as ±1 position.** If your model decision hinges on a two-point gap, you don't have a decision, you have a coin flip.

## Lesson 2: the harness is the benchmark

This is the big one, and it should change how you read every coding leaderboard.

**Claude Opus 4.8 scores 69.2% or 51.9% on SWE-bench depending on which testing harness runs it.** That's a 17.3-point spread — larger than the gap between most models on the chart.

SWE-bench measures *a system*: a model plus a scaffold that decides which files to open, how to retry, when to run tests, how to feed back errors. Swap the scaffold and the number moves more than swapping the model does.

Which means a headline like "K3 beats Fable 5 on Terminal-Bench 88.3 to 84.6" is only meaningful if both ran the same harness at the same settings — and that a 3.7-point gap is well inside harness-variance territory. I made roughly this argument about [why the coding-agent leaderboard lies to you](/blog/terminal-bench-2-1-coding-agent-leaderboard-reality) when Terminal-Bench 2.1 landed, and K3's results are the strongest confirmation yet: the open model won a benchmark, and the benchmark's error bars are wider than the win.

The practical read: **your harness is a bigger lever than your model.** If you're getting mediocre results, changing the scaffold — better context selection, a verification pass, tighter tool definitions — will usually beat swapping to the model one rank higher.

## Lesson 3: hallucination is what the scoring rewards

Back to the 39% → 51% climb.

The definition matters: hallucination rate here is *incorrect answers divided by all non-correct responses*. So it's not "how often is it wrong," it's **"when it's not right, how often does it guess confidently instead of admitting it doesn't know."** Half the time, and rising.

A 2026 Nature study by Kalai et al. explains why this is structural rather than accidental: **dominant headline metrics systematically reward guessing over admitting uncertainty.** Binary grading scores "I don't know" identically to a wrong answer — zero either way. So under standard RL with binary rewards, a model that guesses strictly outscores one that abstains, and hallucination emerges as "a predictable statistical consequence" rather than a random defect.

We are, collectively, training models to bluff, then complaining that they bluff.

For agent work this matters more than for chat, because an agent that guesses doesn't just produce a wrong sentence — it takes a wrong action and then reasons forward from it. That's the phantom-event failure mode from the [agent observability piece](/blog/ai-agent-observability-production-failure-modes) in a different costume: the model handed a bad premise improvises around it rather than failing.

The mitigation isn't model selection, since Fable 5's 54.9% is *worse* than K3's 51%. It's architectural: make the agent revalidate facts against a source before acting, and give it an explicit, rewarded path to say "I need to check." Both are things you build into the harness, not things you pick on a pricing page.

## Lesson 4: the benchmarks are saturating anyway

An ICML study found **29 of 60 benchmarks show high saturation** — frontier models cluster so tightly that the benchmark no longer discriminates. When an open model and two closed frontier models all clear 93% on SWE-bench Verified, that benchmark has stopped being a measurement and become a checkbox.

And the gap that actually matters: researchers report roughly a **37% disparity between lab benchmark scores and real deployment performance**. Generic leaderboards are not sufficient for production evaluation, and at the frontier the bottleneck has shifted from compute to human judgment — someone has to decide what "good" means for your codebase, and no public benchmark encodes that.

## Can you actually run "open weights"?

This is where the story gets less romantic.

| Requirement | Reality |
|---|---|
| Weight download | **1.56 TB** on Hugging Face |
| Aggregate VRAM | **1.6 TB+** |
| Accelerators | **64+** for production serving, newest generations |
| Single 8-GPU node | Not possible — multi-node cluster required |
| Inference | vLLM, with specific handling for Delta Attention |

So "open weights" here means something narrower than it sounds. You can **audit** it, **fine-tune** it if you have a cluster, and — importantly — you're **not locked to one vendor's API**, because anyone can host it. What you almost certainly cannot do is run it yourself. For a small team, self-hosting K3 is not a cost decision, it's a capital expenditure.

If your actual goal is running models on hardware you own, K3 is the wrong end of the telescope — that's the territory I covered in [local-first coding agents with small models](/blog/local-first-coding-agents-small-models-guide), and the economics there are completely different.

The license is permissive with two thresholds worth reading before you build a business on it:

- Free commercial use with attribution; modification and deployment allowed
- **Model-as-a-service businesses above $20M annual revenue** need a separate Moonshot agreement
- **Products above 100M monthly users or $20M monthly revenue** must display "Kimi K3" in the user interface

That second one is unusual and easy to miss in a term sheet. It's not restrictive for most people, but "we must show a competitor's brand in our product UI" is a conversation you want to have with legal before launch, not after.

## Actually using it: it speaks Anthropic

The practical detail that makes K3 immediately relevant rather than academically interesting: **it exposes an Anthropic-compatible endpoint.** Claude Code runs against it with no modification.

```bash
export ANTHROPIC_BASE_URL=https://api.moonshot.ai/anthropic
export ANTHROPIC_AUTH_TOKEN=<your-moonshot-key>
export ANTHROPIC_MODEL=kimi-k3

claude
```

Three environment variables. That's the whole integration. It thinks by default, so you get reasoning traces without extra flags.

Pricing:

| | Rate |
|---|---|
| Input, cache hit | $0.30 / MTok |
| Input, cache miss | $3.00 / MTok |
| Output | $15.00 / MTok |

Moonshot reports **cache hit rates above 90% in coding workloads**, which makes the effective input cost far closer to the $0.30 figure than the $3.00 one. Overall that lands roughly 40% under Opus 4.8.

Two caveats before you rewire your whole workflow. First, you're now sending your code to a different provider under a different data policy — worth the same scrutiny you'd apply to any vendor, and a completely separate question from price. Second, running the Claude Code harness against a non-Anthropic model means Anthropic-specific behaviors won't all carry over; this is the multi-provider reality I mapped out in the [multi-CLI stack guide](/blog/multi-cli-stack-claude-code-codex-gemini-orchestration-guide), and the failure modes are mostly in the seams.

## The limitations Moonshot publishes itself

Credit where it's due — these are in Moonshot's own write-up, not dug out by critics:

1. **Sensitivity to thinking history.** K3 needs a compatible harness. Feed it reasoning traces in a shape it doesn't expect and quality degrades. This is Lesson 2 again, from the model's side.
2. **Excessive proactiveness.** It does more than you asked during task execution. In an agent loop with write access, "helpfully did extra things" is a euphemism for a diff you have to review line by line.
3. **A noticeable user-experience gap** versus Fable 5 and GPT-5.6 Sol. Benchmarks don't measure whether working with a model is pleasant, and Moonshot is honest that this one isn't there yet.

That third admission is worth more than any benchmark in the post. A model that wins on paper and annoys you in practice loses on the only metric that compounds.

## How I'd actually evaluate it

Stop reading leaderboards and build a five-task eval from your own repository. It takes an afternoon and it's the only number that predicts anything:

1. **Pick five real tasks you've already completed** — a bugfix, a refactor, a feature, a test-writing job, a migration. You know what good looks like because you did them.
2. **Run each through your actual harness**, unchanged, on both models. Same scaffold, same prompts, same tools. You're measuring the model, so hold the system fixed.
3. **Score three things**: did it complete the task, how much of the diff did you have to rewrite, and how many turns did it take. The middle one is the one that correlates with how you'll feel in a month.
4. **Track cost per completed task**, not cost per token. A cheap model that needs three attempts isn't cheap — the arithmetic I went through in [cutting a Claude Code bill](/blog/claude-code-token-optimization-bill-cut-guide) applies exactly here.
5. **Re-run it quarterly.** Everything on this page has a shelf life measured in weeks.

## Where it fits

**Use K3** when you're doing high-volume frontend generation (the Frontend Code Arena win is real and it's the most repo-specific benchmark on the list), when cost matters more than polish, or when vendor independence is a genuine requirement rather than a preference.

**Use it as a tier**, not a replacement. This is the strongest case: cheap capable model for routine steps, frontier model for the hard ones. K3 at ~40% under Opus 4.8 makes a compelling middle tier, and the Anthropic-compatible endpoint means routing between them costs you three environment variables rather than a rewrite. That's the [big-agent-vs-micro-agents tiering argument](/blog/big-agent-vs-micro-agents-model-tiering-guide) with a new and unusually well-priced option in the middle.

**Don't self-host it** unless you already operate 64+ accelerators. "Open weights" is about auditability and vendor choice here, not about running it in your garage.

**Don't switch on the benchmark numbers.** A 3.7-point Terminal-Bench gap is inside harness variance, the Intelligence Index ranking depends on how you count effort settings, and the hallucination rate went up as accuracy went up. Run your own five tasks.

The genuinely important thing about K3 isn't its position on any chart. It's that an open-weight model reached the point where the comparison is close enough to require careful measurement — and that our measuring instruments turn out to be the least reliable part of the whole picture.
