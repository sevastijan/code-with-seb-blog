Andrej Karpathy coined "vibe coding" in February 2025. Eighteen months later, at Sequoia's AI Ascent 2026, the same man said: **"I've never felt more behind as a programmer."**

Sit with that for a second. The person who gave the movement its name — the give-in-to-the-vibes, forget-the-code-exists, accept-all-diffs ethos — is now telling a room full of founders that the era he named is basically over, and that he feels like he's falling behind. Not because the tools got worse. Because they got so much better that the *job* changed underneath him, and the skill that matters now is one almost nobody was practicing a year ago.

He has a name for what replaces it: **agentic engineering.** And after spending the last few months rebuilding how I actually work, I think he's right — but the takes flying around are mostly recaps of his talk. Nobody's telling you what this looks like on a real Tuesday. So let me do that.

## What actually changed in December 2025

Karpathy points to a specific inflection: **December 2025** was when agentic coding tools crossed a line from "helpful but messy" to "reliably produces correct chunks of code." That's not a marketing date. Anyone who was running agents through 2025 felt it — the moment the agent's output started, more often than not, exceeding what you'd have written by hand.

That's the uncomfortable core of Karpathy's "behind" comment. It wasn't despair. It was: *the ceiling just moved and I haven't caught up to it.* His exact framing was that he could be "10X more powerful if I just properly string together what has become available over the last ~year." The bottleneck stopped being the model. The bottleneck became **you** — your ability to orchestrate, specify, and verify.

Vibe coding worked when the model was a fast intern you had to babysit. When the intern becomes reliably better than you at writing the actual lines, babysitting is the wrong posture. Directing is the right one. That shift — from typist to director — is the whole ballgame.

## Vibe coding vs agentic engineering: floor vs ceiling

Here's the cleanest distinction I've found, and it's Karpathy's own: **vibe coding raises the floor. Agentic engineering raises the ceiling.**

Vibe coding is about access. You describe what you want, the agent generates it, you accept it and move on. It's genuinely great — it lets a designer ship a working prototype, a PM validate an idea over a weekend, a hobbyist build the tool they always wanted. I've built [a full-stack app in a day this way](/blog/vibe-coding-full-stack-app-one-day-guide), and I'd do it again for a throwaway. The floor is now "anyone can make software."

But vibe coding **delegates ownership** to the AI. You didn't design it, you didn't review it, you didn't test it. That's fine for a demo and catastrophic in production. The technique skips design, skips review, skips testing — and those three skips are exactly what real users, real security requirements, and real scale punish. I've watched vibe-coded features collapse the moment they met traffic, which is why I keep a [vibe coding security audit checklist](/blog/vibe-coding-security-audit-checklist) around for anything that escapes the prototype stage.

Agentic engineering is the opposite bet. You use coding agents as **force multipliers under your direction while you keep full responsibility** for architecture, quality, and judgment. Karpathy put a hard line under it: you are *not allowed to introduce vulnerabilities because of vibe coding.* The agent writes more. You own everything. The ceiling is now "a strong engineer with a good agentic setup peaks well past 10x."

The trap is thinking these are the same activity at different quality levels. They're not. They're different disciplines with different skills.

## Software 3.0: from writing code to writing context

Karpathy's bigger frame is **Software 3.0**. Software 1.0 was code you wrote. Software 2.0 was neural-network weights you trained. Software 3.0 is LLMs — and the crucial claim is that an LLM isn't a faster tool, it's *a new kind of computer.* You program it in English.

Which means the primary artifact of your job is no longer code. It's **context.** Programming shifts from "writing code" to "writing context" — the specs, the constraints, the examples, the guardrails, the memory that steer the agent toward the thing you actually want.

This is not a cute abstraction. It's a concrete practice, and it's why "prompt engineering" undersold it so badly. Managing what the agent knows, in what order, with what priority, is now the highest-leverage thing I do. I went deep on the mechanics of this in my guide to [context engineering in Claude Code](/blog/context-engineering-claude-code-guide) and on using [CLAUDE.md as persistent project memory](/blog/claude-md-memory-persistent-context-guide) — those two files do more for my output than any prompt trick ever did. If you're still optimizing clever one-shot prompts, you're polishing the wrong surface.

## The PEV loop: what my day actually looks like now

The workflow that replaced "prompt and hope" has a shape: **Plan → Execute → Verify.** The PEV loop. Here's how a real feature goes now, start to finish.

**Plan.** I don't prompt the agent to write the feature. I prompt it to write a *plan* for the feature — files it'll touch, the approach, the edge cases, the test strategy — and I read that plan like a senior reviewing a junior's design doc. I kill bad approaches here, where it costs nothing. Claude Code's plan mode formalizes this, and I lean on it hard; I wrote up how I run it in my [plan-mode / ultraplan playbook](/blog/claude-code-plan-mode-ultraplan-production-guide). Ninety percent of agentic failures I see are actually planning failures the person let slide.

**Execute.** The agent writes the code. This is the part that looks like "vibe coding" from the outside and is nothing like it, because it's bounded by a plan I approved and a spec I wrote. For anything non-trivial I fan the work across [parallel subagents](/blog/claude-code-dynamic-workflows-parallel-subagents-guide) — one implements, others review or handle independent slices.

**Verify.** This is where the discipline lives. The agent runs the test suite and loops on failures until they pass. I read the diff — not skim, *read* — for architectural correctness, security, and taste. The agent's job is to make the tests green; my job is to make sure the tests, and the code, are actually right.

The number that captures the shift: working this way, I write something like **5–15% of my code by hand.** The rest is specs, tests, plan reviews, and multi-agent orchestration. If that ratio sounds alarming, notice what it *isn't* — it isn't 5–15% of my *thinking*. The thinking went up. It just moved out of the editor.

## The five skills that now pay

If code volume is no longer the constraint, what is? These five, in rough order of how much they've mattered for me:

1. **Spec design.** Writing requirements detailed enough that an agent can't misinterpret them — but not so rigid they prevent good solutions. This is the new senior skill. The whole discipline of [spec-driven development with tools like Spec Kit](/blog/spec-driven-development-spec-kit-claude-code-guide) exists because a vague spec is now the single most expensive mistake you can make.
2. **Diff review.** Reading generated code fast, for architecture and security, not typos. You need to spot the subtly-wrong abstraction in a 400-line diff in two minutes. This is a *trainable* skill and most people are bad at it because they never had to do it at this volume.
3. **Eval design.** Building feedback loops with verifiable signals so the agent can self-correct. Tests, type checks, lint gates, custom evals — anything that gives the loop a ground truth to close against.
4. **Security oversight.** Because the agent will confidently ship an injection vector, and "the AI wrote it" is not a defense. This is why I treat every agent output as untrusted by default.
5. **Taste.** The unquantifiable one. Knowing what "good" looks like so you can steer toward it. Karpathy's point stands: technical depth didn't become irrelevant, it became a *bigger* multiplier. The deeper you understand systems, the more you can safely delegate — and the faster you spot when the agent is confidently wrong.

Notice none of these is "typing speed." The [honest comparison of Claude Code vs Cursor vs Codex](/blog/claude-code-vs-cursor-vs-codex-honest-developer-comparison-2026) matters less than which of these five skills you've actually built. The tool is a rounding error next to the operator.

## The uncomfortable part: what happens to juniors

I won't pretend this is all upside. Forbes reported in March 2026 that AI agents wrote roughly **80% of Karpathy's code** — and ran the obvious headline: junior developers are paying the price.

Here's the mechanism, and it's real. The traditional path into engineering was: do the grunt work, write the CRUD, fix the small bugs, and absorb judgment by osmosis while doing it. Agentic engineering *deletes the grunt work.* The tasks juniors used to cut their teeth on are now the tasks agents do best and cheapest. So the ladder loses its bottom rungs, and the skills that now pay — spec design, diff review, taste — are precisely the ones you can't have on day one.

I don't have a clean answer, and I distrust anyone who says they do. What I'll say is that "agents don't replace developers" is true but incomplete — the [actual data on that](/blog/ai-agents-not-replacing-developers-real-data) shows augmentation, not replacement, at the aggregate level. But aggregate comfort is cold comfort to a specific junior whose entry-level role just evaporated. The honest version is closer to what I argued in [the uncomfortable truths about AI coding agents](/blog/uncomfortable-truths-ai-coding-agents-2026): the profession isn't dying, but the on-ramp is genuinely being pulled up, and we should say so plainly instead of hiding behind the aggregate.

If you're early-career, the move is brutal but simple: skip the rung that's gone. Don't try to out-type an agent. Get freakishly good at review, specs, and system design *now*, on real projects, using the agents to build things far beyond your years so you accumulate the judgment faster than the ladder was ever going to give it to you.

## How to actually make the shift

Concrete, this week, if you're a working dev still mostly hand-coding:

- **Stop accepting diffs you haven't read.** This one habit is the entire difference between vibe coding and agentic engineering. If you can't review it, you can't own it, and you're vibe coding whether you admit it or not.
- **Write the spec before the prompt.** For your next non-trivial task, write a paragraph of requirements and constraints first. Feed *that*. Watch the quality jump.
- **Make the loop verifiable.** No test suite, no lint gate, no types? Then the agent has nothing to close against and you're the only error-checker in the loop. Fix that first.
- **Plan, then execute.** Ask for the approach before the code. Kill bad plans cheaply.
- **Invest in your setup.** Karpathy's whole "10x" claim hinges on *stringing the tools together well.* Your CLAUDE.md, your subagents, your evals, your memory — that infrastructure is now part of your skill, not a nice-to-have.

## The bottom line

Vibe coding isn't dead — it's demoted. It's the right tool for prototypes, throwaways, and the moment someone who can't code needs to make something real. The floor it raised is a genuinely good thing for the world.

But if you ship software people depend on, the job description quietly rewrote itself. You're not the person who writes the code anymore. You're the person who decides what gets built, specifies it precisely, orchestrates the agents that build it, and takes full responsibility for what ships. That's a harder job than typing, not an easier one — which is exactly why the man who invented vibe coding says he's never felt more behind. The ceiling moved. The only question is whether you move with it.
